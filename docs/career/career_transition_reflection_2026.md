## 5. Meetings, confidence, and professional visibility

- Large meetings involving VPs and product managers feel uncomfortable.
- The user finds it difficult to choose words and articulate technical subjects as fluently as a colleague.
- A higher-up has nominated that colleague to speak because of the colleague's voice and presentation style.
- The colleague regularly speaks English at home, which the user believes helps with spontaneous word choice and fluency.
- The user feels sidelined and less recognized when the colleague receives visibility.
- After some meetings, the user continues thinking about the experience and negatively compares their performance with others.
- The user clarified that this concern is **specific to speaking in high-visibility meetings**, rather than a general lack of confidence while independently solving technical problems.

### Important distinctions

**Technical competence, spoken communication, and professional visibility are different skills and outcomes.** Being chosen as a presenter does not, by itself, prove someone is the strongest engineer or that another person's work is less valuable. Likewise, limited recognition should not be assumed to be deliberate without evidence.

No conclusion was reached about whether upbringing, anxiety, management culture, or other factors are the root cause. Avoid self-diagnosis. If post-meeting distress materially affects daily life, seeking professional support is a reasonable option.

### Practical communication approach

Use **Problem → Action → Impact** for concise technical updates:

- **Problem:** What was wrong or needed improvement?
- **Action:** What was designed, investigated, or changed?
- **Impact:** What improved, and what evidence supports that claim?

When asked something unknown, it is appropriate to say: *“I haven't verified that scenario yet. I'll check and follow up with an accurate answer.”*

Suggested practice: occasional 5–10 minute explanations of familiar engineering work, with one unexpected stakeholder-style question. The goal is clarity and comfort, **not** imitating a colleague's voice, accent, or personality.

### Recognition actions

- Keep a written record of contributions, decisions, and measurable outcomes.
- Request ownership of short technical updates or design summaries for work personally delivered.
- Seek opportunities for written architecture reviews and smaller-group discussions.
- Discuss growth, autonomy, and visibility with the manager without framing it as competition with a colleague.

- Customer-facing solution architecture with frequent executive presentations.
- People-management positions chosen mainly for title or status.

## 7. Financial and lifestyle safeguards

- Aim to retain current take-home income when negotiating; treat **80%** as the absolute minimum, conditional on affordability.
- Compare fixed pay, variable pay, benefits, taxes, and commuting costs rather than CTC alone.
- Before voluntarily resigning without another offer, assess essential monthly expenses and financial runway; 9–12 months of expenses was discussed as a conservative planning target, not a universal requirement.
- Avoid resigning solely because of career stagnation without first testing internal and external options, unless personal circumstances make continued employment untenable.
- A better job title is not worth sacrificing the stated no-on-call and predictable-hours requirements.

## 8. Questions to ask prospective employers

1. Is there **any primary or secondary on-call** responsibility, including major-incident escalation?
2. Who owns production incidents after business hours?
3. How often has the team worked evenings or weekends in the last three months?
4. How much time is spent on planned engineering versus urgent tickets?
5. What are the actual Bengaluru working hours and recurring cross-time-zone meetings?
6. How many office days does the hybrid policy require?
7. How much of the job involves presenting to senior leadership or customers?
8. How are individual technical contributions documented and recognized?
9. How much design autonomy does the engineer have?
10. Are POCs and varied engineering projects part of the normal workload, or exceptions?
11. What does success look like after six months?
12. Why is the position open, and what caused the previous person to leave?

A role should be rejected if confirmed on-call, unacceptable working hours, or pay below the affordable minimum are required, regardless of its overall attractiveness.
